# Experiment 0a: Frozen-World-Model Critic Diagnosis

**Status:** Screen completed; confirmation intentionally not run
**Task:** DMC Cheetah Run, vision observations
**Platform:** CPU
**Purpose:** Diagnostic stress test, not a model of continual learning

## Research question

Can critic-only Imagined Rehearsal (IR), supplied with a competent actor and a
frozen, already trained world model, reconstruct a damaged critic? In addition,
how does imagination horizon trade reward fidelity against latent drift and
compute, and does a damaged actor immediately lose imagined reward coverage
when started from healthy posterior states?

This experiment was deliberately narrower than Continual Imagined Rehearsal
(CIR). It did not train a world model continually, change tasks, or create a
naturally stale critic. Artificial corruption was used to isolate the critic
update and expose failure modes. Its results should therefore guide CIR's
mechanics rather than be treated as a direct test of the CIR hypothesis.

## Experimental design

### Checkpoint and dataset

The source was the converged Cheetah Run vision checkpoint
`checkpoints/cheetah_run_vision_seed0`. A fixed healthy actor collected 12
episodes of 1,001 observations each. The first six episodes supplied rehearsal
anchors and the remaining six were held out for validation. Anchors were
sampled every 10 environment steps.

| Split | Episode returns |
|---|---|
| Rehearsal | 800.6, 818.5, 769.4, 828.4, 579.6, 803.9 |
| Validation | 756.3, 776.8, 828.3, 825.4, 822.5, 818.7 |

The validation mean was approximately 804.7. The dataset therefore mostly
represents competent running while retaining some lower-return states.

For each observation, the dataset stored the real action, reward, episode
flags, and posterior deterministic state, stochastic state, and posterior
logits. This allowed every condition to begin from identical recurrent belief
states without rerunning the environment.

### Critic corruption

Only the online value network was corrupted with a 20% zero mask. The corrupted
online value network was copied into the slow value network so an intact target
could not leak into rehearsal. The actor, encoder, RSSM, decoder, reward head,
and continuation head remained intact.

The perturbation was qualified by comparing its predictions with the healthy
critic on held-out posterior states. Qualification required the value MAE to be
at least 0.25 healthy-value standard deviations.

### Frozen-model fidelity

Recorded healthy actions were replayed open-loop through the frozen world model
at horizons 1, 3, 6, 12, 24, 48, and 96. Predicted rewards and discounted
returns were compared with the corresponding real sequence. The probe also
measured prior-posterior categorical KL, deterministic-state relative L2 drift,
and predicted continuation.

This is an on-dataset fidelity test. It validates the world model under actions
and posterior states generated by the healthy policy; it does not establish
equal accuracy under arbitrary damaged or newly learned policies.

### Proposal-policy coverage

The healthy actor and the existing 20%-zero-mask damaged actor were separately
rolled through the frozen world model from identical healthy posterior
anchors. Sixteen MCPB samples were used per anchor. This compared imagined
return, high-reward coverage, and action diversity without allowing the
damaged actor to affect critic repair.

### Critic repair screen

Critic-only IR was screened at horizons 3, 6, 12, 24, and 48. Every condition
used 128 MCPB posterior samples, 128 critic updates, and rehearsal seeds 0, 1,
and 2.
The actor and world model were hashed after every run to verify that critic
rehearsal had not modified them.

The primary outcome was closure of the initial corrupt-to-healthy critic MAE
gap on held-out anchors:

```text
gap closure = 1 - current MAE / initial MAE
```

A value of 1 denotes complete closure, 0 denotes no change, and a negative
value means the critic became worse.

## Results

### 1. The critic perturbation was far stronger in function space than its
parameter-space label suggested

| Metric | Healthy critic | Corrupted critic |
|---|---:|---:|
| Mean predicted value | 295.85 | 208.98 |
| Value standard deviation | 18.06 | 247.17 |
| MAE from healthy critic | -- | 89.57 |
| RMSE from healthy critic | -- | 261.95 |
| Pearson correlation with healthy critic | -- | 0.039 |
| Spearman correlation with healthy critic | -- | 0.177 |
| Normalized MAE gap | -- | 4.96 |

The corruption passed the lower qualification threshold by a large margin. It
was not a moderate critic mismatch: it produced substantial bias, extreme
outliers, and nearly destroyed value ordering. This matters for external
validity. A critic that becomes stale during ordinary continual adaptation is
likely to remain much closer to its useful behavioural manifold, except
possibly after severe latent representation drift or an abrupt reward change.

The slow and online corrupted critics initially agreed exactly, as intended.

### 2. Reward and return predictions were excellent through the complete
screen range

| Horizon | Return MAE | Return bias | Pearson | NRMSE | Reward-step MAE | Deterministic-state relative L2 | Prior-posterior KL |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 0.011 | 0.003 | 0.998 | 0.071 | 0.011 | 0.000 | 0.082 |
| 3 | 0.034 | 0.010 | 0.998 | 0.072 | 0.012 | 0.115 | 0.098 |
| 6 | 0.067 | 0.020 | 0.997 | 0.074 | 0.012 | 0.184 | 0.117 |
| 12 | 0.130 | 0.040 | 0.997 | 0.075 | 0.012 | 0.296 | 0.149 |
| 24 | 0.268 | 0.085 | 0.997 | 0.083 | 0.014 | 0.465 | 0.176 |
| 48 | 0.707 | -0.101 | 0.992 | 0.125 | 0.022 | 0.819 | 0.294 |
| 96 | 5.874 | -5.661 | 0.926 | 0.576 | 0.088 | 1.362 | 0.807 |

Predicted continuation remained approximately 0.997 throughout. Return
fidelity stayed exceptionally high through horizon 48 even though latent
disagreement accumulated steadily. Horizon 96 showed the first clear aggregate
degradation: a systematic underprediction of return, much larger latent drift,
and weaker within-band ordering.

The reward-band analysis showed the same broad pattern. Absolute errors
remained small in low-, middle-, and high-return regions through horizon 48.
Some within-band correlations and normalized errors looked worse because each
quantile band had little target variance; those statistics should not be read
without the corresponding absolute error.

This provides a useful horizon result independent of critic repair:

- horizon 6 is extremely accurate and cheap but propagates little reward;
- horizons 12 and 24 retain excellent fidelity while exposing more cumulative
  reward;
- horizon 48 remains reward-accurate on this dataset, but its latent trajectory
  has moved substantially away from the subsequently observed posterior; and
- horizon 96 incurs materially greater model bias and approximately twice the
  rollout work of horizon 48.

There is therefore no single universally correct horizon. The useful horizon
depends on whether an update needs local precision, longer reward propagation,
or a particular compute budget. Future CIR experiments should log fidelity by
horizon and may eventually gate horizon using model trust.

### 3. Damaging the actor barely changed imagined reward coverage when both
actors started on the healthy manifold

| Horizon | Healthy imagined return | Damaged imagined return | Mean paired difference |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 0.791 | 0.790 | -0.0004 |
| 3 | 2.367 | 2.366 | -0.0013 |
| 6 | 4.722 | 4.719 | -0.0033 |
| 12 | 9.403 | 9.388 | -0.0149 |
| 24 | 18.622 | 18.557 | -0.0655 |
| 48 | 36.481 | 36.110 | -0.3701 |
| 96 | 69.735 | 68.093 | -1.6426 |

At horizon 6, the mean difference was approximately 0.07%. At horizons 48 and
96 it grew to approximately 1.0% and 2.4%, respectively. The damaged return was
lower on 93% of paired anchors at horizon 48 and every paired anchor at horizon
96, but the absolute separation remained modest.

This does not show that the damaged actor was competent: its real evaluation
performance was much worse. Instead, it suggests that its failure was not
visible as an immediate loss of predicted reward when it was placed back on
healthy posterior states. Small action errors may compound until the real agent
enters poorer beliefs, or the frozen model may be less trustworthy under the
damaged actor's induced distribution. Current-posterior IR is local, so this
distinction remains important for coverage diagnostics in CIR.

### 4. The existing bootstrapped critic objective diverged at every horizon

Median held-out critic MAE across the three rehearsal seeds was:

| Horizon | Update 0 | Update 1 | Update 8 | Update 16 | Update 32 | Update 128 |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 3 | 89.6 | 114.9 | 721.7 | 11,063 | 1,085,498 | 2,331,799 |
| 6 | 89.6 | 113.0 | 874.3 | 20,443 | 1,180,373 | 2,157,323 |
| 12 | 89.6 | 112.7 | 982.1 | 36,220 | 1,395,501 | 1,959,510 |
| 24 | 89.6 | 115.5 | 2,016 | 136,289 | 1,588,096 | 2,142,966 |
| 48 | 89.6 | 145.4 | 5,376 | 380,580 | 1,830,353 | 2,076,016 |

No horizon produced reproducible positive recovery. A few individual seed-2
conditions improved by 1--5% after their first update, but the median worsened
immediately and every run subsequently diverged. Longer horizons accelerated
the early blow-up rather than stabilising it.

The final median gap closures ranged from approximately -21,875 to -26,031.
These values mean errors tens of thousands of times larger than the original
gap, not small negative recovery. The automatic selector chose horizons 6, 12,
and 48 because they were numerically the least negative eligible results. This
selection is not scientifically meaningful, so the planned confirmation stage
was cancelled.

All 15 actor/world-model invariance hashes were identical. Optimizer losses,
gradient norms, and update magnitudes remained finite. The failure was therefore
not caused by actor or model updates, NaNs, or a simple cross-namespace leak.

## Mechanistic interpretation

The rehearsal loss reused DreamerV3's existing imagined lambda-return
objective. In this configuration, `imag_loss.slowtar` is false, so the return
target uses the current online value prediction rather than the slow value
prediction:

```text
target value = current corrupted critic
lambda return = predicted rewards + bootstrapped target values
```

After every optimizer step, the changed critic generates the bootstrap used by
the next step. The reward head was accurate, but its short-horizon reward signal
was small relative to value predictions around 200--300 and the corrupted
critic's extreme errors. The update therefore formed a moving, self-referential
target rather than an independent reconstruction signal.

The value head uses a symlog two-hot distribution. Its loss operates in
compressed symlog space and its scalar prediction is decoded through `symexp`.
A finite categorical shift toward an extreme bin can consequently decode into
values of millions while cross-entropy losses and parameter updates remain
finite. This explains the superficially moderate optimizer losses during the
functional explosion.

The observations are consistent with circular bootstrap instability, but the
experiment did not separately manipulate the bootstrap and therefore does not
by itself prove that it is the only cause. A fixed-target control would be
needed for that causal claim.

## Bootstrap is a design choice, not a requirement

The failed objective is not the only way to train an imagined critic. Given a
trusted reward model, IR can construct an unbootstrapped finite-horizon target:

```text
G_t^(H) = r_t + gamma r_(t+1) + ... + gamma^(H-1) r_(t+H-1)
```

or a trust-weighted tail:

```text
G_t^(H,beta) = finite-horizon rewards
               + beta * gamma^H * V_target(s_(t+H))
```

where `beta=0` ignores the critic tail and `beta=1` recovers ordinary
bootstrapping. `beta` could increase only after critic calibration, or depend on
critic uncertainty and world-model trust.

Removing the bootstrap has consequences that must be explicit:

- the critic estimates an H-step return rather than the approximately
  infinite-horizon value represented by the healthy critic;
- its numerical values therefore should not be judged against the original
  critic unless the objectives are aligned;
- short horizons introduce behavioural myopia;
- long horizons cost more and expose the actor and critic to model drift; and
- reward fidelity measured under the healthy dataset may not transfer to a
  newly changing policy distribution.

For Cheetah Run, the dense reward and strong measured fidelity through horizons
24--48 make an unbootstrapped or partially bootstrapped IR objective especially
plausible. It is an algorithmic opportunity, not merely a workaround for this
stress test.

## What this experiment establishes

1. The frozen Cheetah world model predicts on-dataset rewards and returns with
   high accuracy through horizon 48.
2. Latent prior trajectories drift progressively even while reward predictions
   remain accurate, so reward fidelity and latent fidelity should both be
   reported.
3. Actor damage is not necessarily visible from short rollouts initiated on
   healthy posterior states; behavioural failure can emerge through compounding
   state-distribution drift.
4. The unmodified, self-bootstrapped Dreamer critic loss is not robust to this
   severe critic perturbation when used as repeated standalone rehearsal.
5. Horizon length alone does not resolve a corrupted bootstrap. It changes the
   balance between reward evidence, tail value, compute, and model drift.

## What this experiment does not establish

1. It does not show that critic IR will fail in continual learning.
2. It does not show that CIR requires recovery from a randomly corrupted
   critic. Artificial corruption is not part of the proposed algorithm.
3. It does not establish the best imagination horizon outside this checkpoint,
   task, policy distribution, or world model.
4. It does not test an unbootstrapped, fixed-target, or trust-weighted critic
   objective.
5. It does not test world-model adaptation, representation drift, task changes,
   forgetting, transfer, or compute gating.

The likely continual setting is less extreme: actor and critic should usually
remain near a useful behavioural solution while the predictive model and task
change incrementally. Representation changes can still make a previously good
critic stale, so critic-target trust remains relevant, but this stress-test
failure should not become the centre of the CIR research programme.

## Implications for the next experiments

This diagnostic should now be closed rather than expanded into a large
corruption-recovery study. Its useful engineering lesson is to make the critic
target configurable and measurable. A small safety ablation can compare:

- the existing online bootstrap;
- a fixed slow-target bootstrap;
- no bootstrap; and
- a trust-weighted bootstrap.

That ablation should first verify that critic rehearsal does not destabilise an
intact agent. It need not become the main paper experiment.

The main programme should return to CIR's actual claim: real sequences train a
continual world model, while current-posterior imagination trains behaviour.
The next mainline milestone is a stationary decoupling test, followed by a
controlled task change that creates critic mismatch naturally rather than by
masking weights. Horizon 6 remains the local, cheap reference; horizons 12 and
24 are the most attractive first alternatives; horizon 48 is a useful
high-compute boundary condition.
